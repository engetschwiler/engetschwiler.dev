Tim Ferriss has a question I keep coming back to:

> What is the one decision that, if made, makes all the other decisions easier or irrelevant?

It sounds like a productivity trick. It isn't. Most prioritization methods help you sort your decisions; this one asks you to delete them.

The word doing the heavy lifting is *irrelevant*. Easier is nice. Irrelevant is better, because a decision you never have to make again costs you nothing, forever.

I've been running Bee Interactive on my own since 2011, straight out of school. When I look back at those fifteen years through this question, the choices that saved me the most energy weren't the big projects. They were a few quiet decisions that made dozens of others disappear.

## Dominoes I've already knocked over

### One stack

I picked Laravel back in the 4.2 days and never seriously looked back. That single choice answered hundreds of questions before they were asked: which framework, which ORM, which queue system, how to deploy, what to learn next.

It also made me better. Fifteen years in one ecosystem means I'm not relearning the basics on every project. When a client asks something new, I'm extending what I know instead of starting over.

### My own CMS

At some point I stopped choosing a CMS per project and built my own, [Lochness](/articles/2025/12/09/lochness-cms-why-i-built-my-own-laravel-powered-cms). One decision, and suddenly every content-driven site starts from the same foundation, with the same back-office my clients already know how to use.

### Building tools, not just websites

The biggest one: deciding that what I do best is business tools. CRMs, internal platforms, member portals, the kind of software that replaces a spreadsheet nobody wants to touch anymore.

Once that was clear, a lot of questions answered themselves. Which leads to chase, what to show in my portfolio, which projects to politely decline, what to write about. It even shaped how I deliver: the code belongs to the client, with full Git access, hosted in Switzerland. I don't debate that anymore. It's just how it works.

### Pitching in person

I stopped sending cold quotes. When a project matters, I present the concept and the offer face to face. That one rule made a whole category of anxious follow-up emails irrelevant, because the questions get answered in the room.

## The bike knew it first

[Long-distance cycling](/articles/2026/07/02/what-long-distance-cycling-taught-me-about-being-a-better-developer) taught me this before business did. In 2015, a friend came back from three years of riding around the world. I was at home, not doing much, and thought: why not me?

Nordkapp was the obvious target, a kind of pilgrimage for touring cyclists. So I picked a date, May 29, gave myself a couple of months, and that was it. No training plan, no detailed route: I packed the bike and the panniers, and left from my front door.

Picking that date didn't make the other decisions easier. It made most of them unnecessary. On the road, only small questions were left: how far today (usually 60 to 100 km, depending on the weather), and where to sleep tonight, whether a campsite, an Airbnb, a hot shower at a local's place, wild camping or a hotel.

I'm the kind of person who always has a plan B, and I had one for every tricky situation. I never needed a single one. A few times I hesitated, took another road, turned back and went anyway. The route changed, sometimes by train, once with a short flight. The destination never did.

It took me roughly six weeks to reach the globe at the North Cape. Nothing heroic, no record, no drama. Just a deep sense of pride at having got there with my own legs and my own ingenuity.

![The metal globe monument at the North Cape against a clear blue sky](/img/articles/2026-11-20-the-one-decision-that-makes-the-others-irrelevant/north-cape-globe.jpg "The North Cape globe, 2015")

What I brought back was confidence. When there's a wall in front of me now, I remember that I rode to the North Cape almost on a whim, and I know I can handle it. That one decision is still knocking over dominoes a decade later.

## When the question doesn't help

I like this question a lot, but it has limits.

- **There isn't always one domino.** Sometimes there are just several decisions to make, one after the other. Hunting for the magic one can become an elegant way of not deciding anything.
- **It's clearer in hindsight.** Everything I listed above looks obvious now. At the time, each of those choices felt like one option among many.
- **It works best on repetition.** If you keep making the same small decision every week, there's probably a rule hiding behind it. For a one-off decision, the question adds little.

I also have to be honest about my side projects. I build a lot of apps for myself, and every new idea is a perfect excuse to postpone the real question: which one actually matters this quarter? Picking one would make the others irrelevant. That's exactly why it's hard.

## The one I'm still looking for

Right now, my open domino is about direction. I know what I do and who I do it for, but I don't yet have a sentence sharp enough to settle every "should I post about this?", "should I take this project?" or "should I build this?" in one go.

I suspect that once I find it, a lot of my current to-do list will quietly become irrelevant. That's how you recognize a lead domino: not by how much work it creates, but by how much it removes.

So here's the question back to you. What's the one decision you've been circling around that would make the rest of your list easier, or let you delete it?
